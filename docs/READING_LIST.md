# Domain Reading List — OrbitWarden

> Inline summaries so you can get 80% of the value in 2–3 hours. Links are for going deeper on anything that feels shaky before Phase 1.

## 1. TLEs and SGP4 propagation

**What a TLE is:** a Two-Line Element set encodes a satellite's *mean* orbital elements (inclination, RAAN, eccentricity, argument of perigee, mean anomaly, mean motion) plus a drag term (BSTAR) and an **epoch** (the timestamp the elements are valid for). TLEs are generated by fitting observations — they are not instantaneous state vectors.

**SGP4:** the standard propagator for TLEs (Vallado's revision of Spacetrack Report #3). Uses analytic perturbations (J2 oblateness, drag) rather than numerical integration. Fast (~100k evals/s in the C-accelerated Python library) but accuracy degrades with time from epoch — **typically ~1 km/day in LEO, worse during storms**. Both your satellite and the debris are propagated with the same model, so *relative* geometry is better than absolute positions — this is why screening works.

**Frame:** SGP4 outputs TEME (True Equator Mean Equinox). For conjunction screening you never need to leave TEME — distances between two TEME states are exact. (We loosely call it "J2000-ish" in the UI; document the approximation in `ASSUMPTIONS.md`.)

**Key insight for us:** TLE staleness is our biggest fidelity limit — and the storm flag (§5) is the honest, operationally-correct way to surface it.

- python-sgp4 library: https://pypi.org/project/sgp4/ — includes Vallado's verification test cases (we use them in Phase 1 tests)
- Vallado, "Revisiting Spacetrack #3" (AIAA 2006-6753) — the definitive reference, free PDF via CelesTrak
- CelesTrak TLE FAQ: https://celestrak.org/columns/v04n01/

## 2. Conjunction screening

**The problem:** propagate your satellite and every catalog object over a window (we use 7 days), find close approaches. Naive = N×10,080 SGP4 evaluations per object. The standard trick (what we implement):

1. **Altitude-band pre-filter:** from each TLE compute perigee/apogee radii (`a(1±e)`, with `a` from mean motion). Keep only objects whose altitude band overlaps yours within a margin M (~150 km; widen during storms). Cuts ~28k → ~2–5k.
2. **Coarse scan:** 60 s time steps; find distance local minima below a threshold (~100 km).
3. **Fine refinement:** golden-section/Brent minimization around each candidate → precise TCA and miss vector.

**B-plane:** the plane perpendicular to the relative velocity vector at TCA. The miss vector projected onto it is what collision probability is computed in. Components in RSW (Radial / in-track / cross-track) tell you the approach geometry: in-track-dominated encounters are the common, more predictable kind; radial-dominated are rarer and harder.

**The incumbent to know:** CelesTrak **SOCRATES** (Satellite Orbital Conjunction Reports Assessing Threatening Encounters in Space) — free, public, daily close-approach lists. We cross-validate against it in Phase1 and differentiate from it in the pitch: SOCRATES gives you a table of miss distances; OrbitWarden gives you a judgment layer + maneuver options + a maneuver card.

- SOCRATES: https://celestrak.org/SOCRATES/
- CCSDS Conjunction Data Message (green book overview): https://public.ccsds.org/

## 3. Collision probability (the formula we implement)

Short-term2-D encounter probability (**Alfriend–Foster**, 1992) — valid when the encounter is fast relative to orbital curvature (true for LEO conjunctions at ~10–15 km/s):

```
Pc ≈ (HBR² / (2·σx·σy)) · exp( −xm²/(2σx²) − ym²/(2σy²) )
```

- `(xm, ym)` — miss vector on the B-plane
- `HBR` — hard-body radius = (size₁ + size₂)/2 from SATCAT (default 50 m when unknown)
- `σx, σy` — projected combined position uncertainty

**The covariance problem:** real Pc needs each object's true covariance (from tracking radar quality) — only CDM issuers have that. **Our MVP fix: a fixed, documented diagonal covariance in RSW (σ_in-track = 1.0 km, σ_radial = σ_cross = 0.5 km), stated in the UI.** This makes Pc a *display* quantity; **ranking is driven by miss distance + relative velocity + geometry**, which don't depend on covariance at all. The CDM replay (Phase 4) empirically validates whether our ranking agrees with reality.

- Alfriend & Foster, "Collision Probability for Spacecraft" (AAS 92-141) — original paper
- Vallado, "Collision Probability and Risk Assessment" chapter in *Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications* — the standard textbook treatment

## 4. CDM — Conjunction Data Message (our validation gold)

A CCSDS-standard message issued when a tracked conjunction crosses a screening threshold. Fields we care about: TCA, miss distance, relative speed, Pc, object IDs, and (in full CDMs) covariances. **Space-Track publishes `CDM_PUBLIC`** — a subset of real historical CDMs — which lets us replay real events through our engine and measure: did we detect it? what rank? how close was our predicted miss to the CDM-stated miss? **This one-page report is the single highest-credibility artifact in the whole submission.**

- Space-Track CDM_PUBLIC docs: https://www.space-track.org/documentation#/cdm
- CCSDS CDM standard (blue book): https://public.ccsds.org/Pubs/508x0b1e2c2.pdf

##5. Space weather (the storm flag)

Geomagnetic storms (driven by CMEs/solar wind) heat and expand the upper atmosphere → **drag increases → LEO orbits decay faster and TLE predictions diverge from reality**. Quantified by the **Kp index** (0–9; ≥6 = storm) and NASA **DONKI** notifications (Geomagnetic Storm Notifications, Warnings).

**Operational consequence:** any conjunction whose prediction window straddles a storm has inflated uncertainty → the correct action is "re-screen ≤ 24 h before TCA," not "trust this miss distance." Implementing this flag is cheap (one forecast fetch + a date comparison) and demonstrates genuine space-ops sophistication most entries will miss.

- NOAA SWPC 3-day Kp forecast (JSON, no auth): https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/products/noaa-planetary-k-index-forecast.json
- NASA DONKI: https://api.nasa.gov (notifications, type=GST)

## 6. Maneuver basics (shoot-and-score)

**Impulsive burn approximation:** for small Δv (< ~1 m/s) the burn is modeled as instantaneous. We apply Δv in RSW components to the state at the burn epoch and re-propagate — "shoot-and-score": measure the resulting miss distance at the original TCA. No analytic optimization needed; a grid of 5 epochs × 5 directions × 6 magnitudes = 150 candidates is trivial compute.

**Rocket equation:** propellant cost `Δm = m·(1 − exp(−Δv/(g₀·Isp)))`, g₀ = 9.80665 m/s². Mass and Isp are **user inputs** (a CubeSat with cold-gas thrusters: Isp ~ 50–70 s; many CubeSats have *no* propulsion — the tool must handle "no maneuver capability" gracefully: then the output is triage + tracking advice, not a burn).

**Typical numbers:** LEO avoidance burns are usually 1–20 cm/s. Burning earlier is cheaper (more time for a small Δv to shift the along-track position) but commits sooner with worse ephemeris data — that's exactly the tradeoff the AI explains to the operator.

**Our wedge:** the *judgment layer* — plain-English triage, constraint-aware maneuver selection, generated maneuver cards, what-if reasoning — for the operators who can't afford COMSPOC. Position explicitly as **decision support and education, never autonomous ops** (human always in the loop).
